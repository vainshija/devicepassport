Where Do Devices Come From?

Globalisation, medical devices and India

Does globalisation make healthcare technology more accessible, or more unequal? This page follows a medical device from the laboratory to the patient, one question at a time, and puts the data behind each step in your hands.

Each question has a control to try. Using it stamps your passport at the top of the page. Collect all seven stamps and the passport is issued.

PrologueWürzburg, Paris, Calcutta · 1895 to 1896

The idea travelled in seven months. The tube is still travelling.

In Würzburg, in the winter of 1895, Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays and refused to patent them. On 23 June 1896, Mahendra Lal Sircar produced India's first X-ray photograph at the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science in Calcutta. The idea had arrived almost at once. The glass tube that made the image came from Ducretet, an instrument maker in Paris.

India now assembles imaging machines of its own, yet the X-ray tube and the detector, the two costliest parts, are still imported. This page asks whether globalisation has closed that gap or kept it open.

What counts as a medical device

The World Health Organization defines a device as any instrument, machine, implant, in vitro reagent, software or material intended for a medical purpose. It estimates about two million kinds on the market, in more than 7,000 generic groups. A medicine acts pharmacologically, immunologically or metabolically; a device does not.

Two words we keep apart

Access: whether patients can obtain and afford a technology, following Penchansky and Thomas (1981). Capability: whether a country can make, repair and improve it. Globalisation can raise one while leaving the other where it was.

Question one
Question oneGlobal production networks

Where are healthcare technologies developed, made and supplied?

In very few places. Four economies, the European Union, the United States, China and Switzerland, ship 72 per cent of the world's health-product exports, and four buy 58 per cent of its imports.

Richard Baldwin's The Great Convergence explains the pattern. Around 1990, cheap communication let firms split production across borders while keeping know-how inside their own networks. Each hub now owns a different step of the chain, and value collects where the knowledge sits.

Question two
Question twoTrade and multinational firms

How do trade and multinationals shape availability and price?

Trade in health goods works less like a world market than a club. High-income economies are 41 per cent of reporting countries but make 86 per cent of exports and take 83 per cent of imports. Among their top ten named partners, the largest exporters sell nothing to lower-middle or low-income countries.

Developing-country suppliers do take part, but they sell upwards. In 2022 China sold 41.4 billion dollars of medical products to the United States, Mexico 18.5 billion and India 10.4 billion. India is the only lower-middle-income economy in any WTO top-20 exporter list.

Question three
Question threeIntellectual property

Do patents reward innovation but restrict access?

For medicines, India's own history answers yes. Its Patents Act of 1970 protected processes, not drugs, so Indian firms could legally make cheaper versions of patented molecules. When generic competition reached HIV treatment, the price fell by more than 99 per cent.

Devices behave differently. A complex device may carry many patents, but designs can be invented around and products last 18 to 24 months. For devices, the harder barrier is the capability to build.

Information technology shrinks distance, so a blueprint travels in seconds. Producing at speed needs suppliers and skills that do not travel with it.

Question four
Question fourTechnology transfer

Does international collaboration help poorer countries build capability?

Mostly it has delivered objects rather than skills. About 80 per cent of health technology in low-resource settings has been donated, and around 40 per cent of equipment there is out of service.

A WHO study in Ethiopia, Nigeria, South Africa and Tanzania found local production technically feasible for many devices, with candidates averaging 80 to 86 per cent on its feasibility index against a 75 per cent threshold. Yet 74 per cent of the 205 people surveyed had no experience of technology transfer.

Bengaluru, around 2008: the MAC 400

A GE team with eight of its nine engineers in India designed a portable ECG sold at a third of the price of imported systems. GE sold 7,500, about 2,000 of them in India. The knowledge was real, and it stayed inside GE.

Cape Town, 2021: the mRNA hub

The WHO's technology-transfer hub at Afrigen, launched in June 2021, had 14 manufacturing partners in 14 countries by May 2025. It is a deliberate attempt to move capability rather than boxes.

What the data showCollaboration has mostly moved objects. Where knowledge does move, it moves inside the firm that owns it.
Course lens

Baldwin, qualified

Know-how reached India through GE's pipeline, but ownership did not travel with it. The factory economy hosts; the headquarter economy owns.

Course lens

Williamson and Myrdal

Asset specificity explains why makers keep control of service: independent Indian repairers get only 5 to 10 per cent of spare parts directly from manufacturers. Cumulative causation explains why capacity flows to where capacity already is.

Another reading

Local production passed WHO's feasibility test. The obstacles named were business, supply chain and governance, which policy can change.

Question five
Question fiveDependence on foreign suppliers

What happens when countries depend on foreign suppliers?

Dependence comes in layers. Only a handful of economies, the EU, Switzerland, India, Israel and Singapore, are net exporters of medicines; almost everyone else buys. In the Central African Republic, Burundi and Malawi, health goods take about a tenth of all imports.

Poorer countries also tend to tax what they import. A WTO study estimated that tariffs on mosquito nets cost sub-Saharan Africa three million nets between 2011 and 2015.

Heckscher-Ohlin

A labour-abundant economy exports labour-intensive goods and buys capital- and knowledge-intensive ones. In normal times that is efficient specialisation. In a crisis it becomes exposure.

Question six
Question sixCOVID-19 as a case study

What did COVID-19 reveal about globalised healthcare production?

First, strength. Global factories scaled up fast. World exports of COVID-19 medical products rose from 738 to 899 billion dollars in 2020, and the surge came mostly from China, which Baldwin now calls the world's sole manufacturing superpower.

Then, weakness. By April 2020 about 80 economies had restricted exports of medical goods, and only 13 had notified the WTO. Vaccines were later allocated by income.

Question seven
Question sevenIndia's position

Is India a beneficiary of globalisation, or still dependent?

Downstream, India is a clear beneficiary. It makes about 83 per cent of lower-middle-income exports of these goods, ranks first in its income group in all eight product categories, and supplies roughly a fifth of the world's generic medicines by volume.

Upstream, it is dependent. About 80 per cent of the medical devices sold in India are imported, and device imports rose 17 per cent to about ₹89,000 crore in 2025-26, despite the 2023 National Medical Devices Policy.

Our answer

Globalisation has made healthcare technology more available, and far cheaper wherever competition or the state stepped in. But the steps that carry the most value, research, core components and finance, stay in a few hubs, and everyone else is plugged in on their terms. We call this dependent integration.

India sits on both sides of it: a beneficiary as a maker of medicines and a buyer of devices, dependent for the technologies and inputs that matter most. In 1896 the idea reached Calcutta in seven months. The tube is still on its way.

Limits: trade values are nominal US dollars and reflect market size as well as need; access data are snapshots from different survey years; foreign investment is inferred from export concentration and ownership shares rather than measured directly; the income-group trade figures use a different country sample from country-level series.
